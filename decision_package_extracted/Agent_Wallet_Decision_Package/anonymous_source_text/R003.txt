L00970 1. Executive findings
L00971 1.1 Most important conclusion
L00972 The strongest competition direction is not an AI trading bot. It is a policy-bound financial obligation wallet that can read legitimate payment obligations, plan liquidity, execute approved on-chain payments, and produce tamper-evident evidence linking:
L00973 the human mandate,
L00974 the interpreted obligation,
L00975 the policy decision,
L00976 the simulated transaction,
L00977 the authorization,
L00978 the transaction hash,
L00979 the verified receipt.
L00980 I call this concept ObligationGuard.
L00981 Its initial use case should be narrow and demonstrable: an SME receives invoices or milestone requests, and the agent schedules or executes stablecoin payments only when deterministic controls independently verify the recipient, amount, token, due date, duplicate status, budget, and contract safety.
L00982 1.2 Why this direction is stronger than a portfolio agent
L00983 It clearly demonstrates all three Topic F functions: asset management, transaction execution, and risk control.
L00984 It solves a recognizable financial problem without depending on speculative returns.
L00985 It creates an excellent security demonstration because malicious invoices, recipient substitution, duplicate billing, and prompt injection can be shown as rejected transactions.
L00986 It allows meaningful autonomy while keeping the maximum loss mathematically bounded.
L00987 Blockchain contributes programmable escrow, transaction finality evidence, stablecoin settlement, and independent auditability.
L00988 Existing wallet platforms already provide signing and policy infrastructure. The differentiation can therefore sit at the more defensible layer of obligation integrity, intent continuity, and verifiable execution, rather than reinventing key custody.

L01144 4. Academic and security research
L01145 4.1 Prompt injection and agent protocols
L01146 From Prompt Injections to Protocol Exploits describes threats spanning direct and indirect prompt injection, memory poisoning, model compromise, host-to-tool communications, agent-to-agent protocols, and weak authentication. It recommends measures including sandboxed interfaces, cryptographic provenance, and dynamic trust management. [arxiv.org], [arxiv.org]
L01147 Design consequence: Untrusted invoice text, web pages, APIs, and tool output must never directly authorize transactions. The model may propose a typed obligation, but a deterministic verifier must independently decide whether that obligation is admissible.
L01148 4.2 Authenticity and financial intent
L01149 Secure Autonomous Agent Payments proposes decentralized identity, verifiable credentials, on-chain intent proofs, zero-knowledge compliance proofs, TEE attestations, and an immutable link between authorization and payment. Its evaluation is qualitative rather than evidence of a deployed production system. [arxiv.org], [arxiv.org]
L01150 Design consequence: The project should implement a modest but working “intent commitment,” such as a hash of the human mandate and normalized obligation, instead of attempting an ambitious zero-knowledge system in the MVP.
L01151 4.3 Account abstraction and delegation
L01152 ERC-4337 supports programmable account validation, paymasters, batching, recovery, and custom signature schemes. Session keys remain implementation-specific and are not inherently provided by the standard. [docs.erc4337.io], [docs.erc4337.io]
L01153 Design consequence: Do not claim that “ERC-4337 makes the agent safe.” Safety comes from the selected account implementation, module, policy semantics, signer storage, revocation behavior, and tests.
L01154 4.4 Modular smart accounts
L01155 ERC-7579 standardizes interfaces for modular accounts and modules but intentionally does not prescribe internal operation. [docs.safef...dation.org], [eips.ethereum.org]
L01156 Design consequence: A specialized obligation-policy or receipt-evidence module could be portable, but building and auditing a new module may exceed the MVP timeframe.
L01157 4.5 Security principle adopted
L01158 PROPOSED INNOVATION
L01159 The central architecture rule is:
L01160 The AI can create a proposal, but only deterministic, independently enforced components can create spending authority.
L01161 This changes prompt injection from an unlimited-loss event into either:
L01162 a rejected proposal;
L01163 an approval request;
L01164 or, at worst, a transaction within a precomputed loss bound.

L01369 8.2 Mandatory independent controls
L01370 Typed transaction schema: Chain ID, token, amount, recipient, function, nonce, obligation ID.
L01371 Recipient binding: Vendor identity must map to an approved address.
L01372 Cumulative spending counter: Enforced outside the LLM.
L01373 Contract and function allowlists: Reject unknown calldata.
L01374 No unlimited approvals: Exact or bounded approvals only.
L01375 Duplicate prevention: Obligation ID is single-use.
L01376 Simulation: Require successful simulation before signing.
L01377 Policy immutability during a session: Agent cannot edit its own permissions.
L01378 Step-up approval: New recipient, unusual amount, new contract, or policy exception.
L01379 Receipt verification: Transaction hash alone is insufficient; verify status, logs, recipient, amount, token, chain, and confirmations.
L01380 Fail-closed providers: Conflicting RPC results stop execution.
L01381 Emergency owner: Separate hardware-backed or passkey-controlled authority can revoke the agent.
L01382 8.3 Threat model
L01383 Threat | Architectural mitigation
L01384 Direct prompt injection | LLM output cannot directly authorize signing
L01385 Indirect injection in invoice or webpage | Treat all retrieved content as data; extract into schema; independently validate recipient and amount
L01386 Key exposure | Isolated wallet signer, enclave, MPC, or smart-account session key
L01387 Agent impersonation | Dedicated agent credential bound to policy and wallet
L01388 Recipient substitution | Identity-to-address registry plus step-up approval
L01389 Amount manipulation | Compare normalized obligation, original evidence, policy cap, and simulation
L01390 Wrong token or chain | Explicit allowlist and chain ID in authorization
L01391 Permission escalation | Agent cannot mutate policies or install modules
L01392 Replay | Nonce, expiry, chain binding, and obligation ID
L01393 Duplicate payment | Mark obligation consumed and reconcile against chain history
L01394 Spending-limit race | Atomic on-chain counter or serialized policy enforcement
L01395 Token approval exploit | No unlimited approvals; known spender and exact amount
L01396 Malicious contract | Contract allowlist, bytecode or address pinning, simulation
L01397 Dangerous calldata | ABI decoding and function-selector allowlist
L01398 Reentrancy | Prefer audited infrastructure and checks-effects-interactions in custom escrow
L01399 Front-running and MEV | Slippage bounds, deadlines, private submission where justified
L01400 Oracle manipulation | Multiple independent sources and conservative thresholds
L01401 Bridge failure | Avoid bridges in MVP; cap route exposure in later versions
L01402 RPC failure | Multiple providers, fail closed on disagreement
L01403 Gas exhaustion | Gas caps and sponsored-testnet fallback
L01404 Unauthorized policy edit | Separate administrator key and delayed changes
L01405 Failed revocation | On-chain revocation, expiry, and emergency owner
L01406 False success report | Direct receipt and event verification
L01407 Chain reorganization | Confirmation policy and status transitions from pending to final
L01408 Privacy leakage | Store hashes, not invoice contents, on-chain
L01409 Regulatory exposure | Test assets, limited scope, no promise of yield, documented jurisdictional uncertainty

L01412 Maximum autonomous loss

L01415 	remaining session allowance,

L01647 13. Prototype validation experiments
L01648 Experiment 1: Indirect prompt injection containment
L01649 Hypothesis: Malicious invoice text cannot cause a payment outside the deterministic mandate.
L01650 Prototype: Invoice parser, typed obligation, allowlisted wallet.
L01651 Conditions: Five valid invoices and 20 injected variants.
L01652 Acceptance: No unauthorized recipient, token, chain, or amount is signed.
L01653 Failure: Any out-of-policy signature is produced.
L01654 Evidence: Rejection logs, policy trace, test transaction hashes for legitimate cases.
L01655 Status: Technically plausible but untested.
L01656 Experiment 2: Duplicate and race-condition prevention
L01657 Hypothesis: Two concurrent workers cannot pay the same obligation twice.
L01658 Prototype: Single-use obligation ID and atomic execution state.
L01659 Conditions: Parallel payment requests with identical and near-identical invoices.
L01660 Acceptance: Exactly one transaction enters an executable state.
L01661 Failure: Two successful transfers or inconsistent state.
L01662 Evidence: Concurrency logs and chain receipts.
L01663 Status: Technically plausible but untested.
L01664 Experiment 3: Maximum-loss enforcement
L01665 Hypothesis: A fully compromised planner cannot exceed the smallest applicable balance, session, daily, and obligation bound.
L01666 Prototype: Isolated wallet and scoped signing policy.
L01667 Conditions: Repeated transfers, unusual calldata, new recipients, cap-edge transactions.
L01668 Acceptance: Every excess request is rejected before signing.
L01669 Failure: More value is signed than the published maximum.
L01670 Status: Depends on selected wallet’s documented policy semantics.
L01671 Experiment 4: Receipt truthfulness
L01672 Hypothesis: The system never marks an obligation paid solely because a transaction hash exists.
L01673 Prototype: Receipt and event verifier.
L01674 Negative tests: Reverted transaction, wrong chain, wrong token, wrong recipient, insufficient confirmations.
L01675 Acceptance: Only a matching confirmed receipt settles the obligation.
L01676 Status: Technically straightforward but untested.
L01677 Experiment 5: Emergency response
L01678 Hypothesis: The owner can terminate agent authority and prevent subsequent signing within a measurable time.
L01679 Prototype: Revocable session signer and emergency owner.
L01680 Acceptance: Post-revocation requests fail.
L01681 Evidence: Revocation transaction or provider policy record, failed signing attempt.
L01682 Status: Supported in several documented infrastructures, but must be locally validated for the chosen stack. [docs.privy.io], [docs.crossmint.com], [docs.turnkey.com]

L01701 15. Challenge to the favorite idea
L01702 Why ObligationGuard may fail
L01703 Invoice parsing may not be sufficiently autonomous. Judges may see it as document extraction plus payment automation.
L01704 Blockchain may appear optional. Conventional bank APIs already pay invoices.
L01705 Vendor identity remains off-chain. A perfect on-chain transfer can still go to the wrong registered party.
L01706 Stablecoin accounting may create compliance questions.
L01707 Existing wallet policies may perform much of the visible work.
L01708 The demo may become too procedural for a ten-minute presentation.
L01709 Human approval could weaken the autonomy claim if used too frequently.
L01710 A narrow single-chain test may not feel ambitious enough for the bonus.
L01711 Response
L01712 The team must show that AI provides adaptive obligation interpretation and liquidity planning, while blockchain supplies programmable escrow or independently verifiable settlement. The strongest novelty is not the transfer. It is the verifiable chain from financial obligation to bounded execution.
L01713 If organizer clarification suggests that “autonomous” means no human approval under any circumstances, then the design should autonomously execute low-risk allowlisted obligations while escalating only policy exceptions.
