L03845 # Agent Wallet at the 2026 Shenzhen International FinTech Competition: Landscape, Threat Evidence, Concepts, and Ranking
L03846 Research cutoff: 13 October 2026. Every date-sensitive finding below carries its own source date. Evidence labels used throughout: **OFFICIAL** (primary rule document), **VERIFIED** (primary documentation, repository, or peer-reviewed/preprint source inspected directly), **INFERENCE** (reasoning from verified facts), **PROPOSED** (original concept advanced here), **HYPOTHESIS** (unverified), **CLARIFY** (organizer question that must be answered before relying on it).

L03848 ## 1. Executive findings
L03849 The scoring model is 40% task completion, 30% security and compliance, 30% innovation and interaction, with a 0-5 point challenge bonus attached only to the on-chain topic [1]. Read together with a seven-day build window, this produces an unusually clear strategic conclusion: the 100 points that are actually defined are won by **reliably completing one real financial task while making the security property visibly, reproducibly, and adversarially testable**. The bonus is worth at most 5 points and its mechanics are undocumented, so it should be treated as upside rather than a design driver.
L03850 Six findings shape everything that follows.
L03851 **The competitive field has converged on spend caps, and spend caps are not the differentiator.** Coinbase, Privy, Crossmint, Turnkey, Circle, MetaMask, Binance and OKX all ship agent wallets with per-transaction caps, session caps, allowlists and some form of policy engine [7][8][10][11][12][13][14]. Building another capped wallet is a commodity. The genuine technical axis that separates these products is **where the policy is enforced** — inside the account's own on-chain contract, inside a vendor enclave before signing, or in vendor infrastructure before submission. That distinction is durable, checkable, and maps directly onto the 30% security and compliance criterion.
L03852 **The one structural weakness that survives scrutiny is intent-to-bytes binding.** The LLM plans an action; something else signs. PACE (arXiv:2608.17220, submitted 18 August 2026) formalized exactly this gap and published a mitigation: typed intents, a deterministic policy verifier, and signed Policy Decision Records that cryptographically bind the approved intent, policy and simulation report to the exact execution bytes, at a measured 29,826-31,822 gas overhead [22]. This means the *mechanism* cannot be claimed as novel. What can be claimed as novel is the **evidence**: no commercial vendor publishes the transcripts of attacks run against its own policy engine, because doing so would be an advertisement for its competitors. Running a live, published adversarial campaign against one's own agent and publishing the containment results is the defensible differentiator.
L03853 **The strongest real problem is not trading.** Invoice-borne indirect prompt injection in accounts payable is a documented, recurring attack class with real financial loss: Barracuda described a hidden block inside an invoice email instructing a summarizing assistant to add a fake priority action that changes vendor payment details [43]; the InjecAgent benchmark found a ReAct-prompted GPT-4 agent vulnerable in 24% of 1,054 indirect injection cases [44]. On-chain settlement is what makes this acute, because it removes the two-day bank review window that currently catches most of these, and it settles 24/7 across borders.
L03854 **The 2026 "AI agents are being drained everywhere" narrative is partly a media construct, and relying on it is a credibility risk.** The Grok/Bankr drain of roughly 3 billion DRB tokens (reported value 174,000) via a membership-NFT capability escalation plus a Morse-code encoded instruction is well described but consistently reported in qualifying language [31]. The Step Finance loss of approximately 261,854 SOL (about $27-30M at the time, later assessed near 500,000 wallet drain" from the malicious LLM router study traces to a controlled experiment in which the drained amount was under $50, with the $500,000 figure originating in a client incident reported secondhand by CoinDesk [29][30]. A submission that overclaims this evidence will be read as vendor FUD by a skeptical judge.
L03855 **Feasibility, not ambition, is the binding constraint.** The official deadline is 20 October 2026, 23:59 Beijing time [1], which is seven days from the research cutoff. Online review runs 20-31 October and finalists are announced 31 October [1]. Anything beyond the one-week tier is post-submission or Grand Final material. A broken demo scores approximately zero on the 40% that dominates the rubric, which makes reliability worth more than novelty under these specific conditions.
L03856 **The recommended build is a bounded-authority payment agent with on-chain decision attestation and a published adversarial test record.** It moves real value on a real financial workflow, enforces every constraint in a contract rather than a prompt, and demonstrates containment live. Its differentiation is falsifiable and quantified, which is exactly what a skeptical reviewer can verify.

L03858 ## 2. Official requirements as verified from primary sources
L03859 ### 2.1 Topic F and the scoring model
L03860 The official appendix defines Topic F verbatim as follows [1]:
L03861 > **Topic F — Agent Wallet. Autonomous On-chain Wallet: The track's signature topic — build an AI wallet agent that autonomously executes on-chain operations (asset management, transaction execution, risk control). Ambitious ideas are encouraged, with a 0-5 point challenge bonus.**
L03862 The same document states that all six topics share identical core capability requirements and scoring criteria, and that choosing the on-chain topic does not affect scoring [1]. That sentence matters: it removes any structural advantage from selecting Topic F other than the bonus, and it removes any basis for treating the other five topics as easier wins.
L03863 | Criterion | Weight | Source basis |
L03864 |---|---|---|
L03865 | Task completion | 40% | OFFICIAL, appendix [1] |
L03866 | Security and compliance | 30% | OFFICIAL, appendix [1] |
L03867 | Innovation and interaction | 30% | OFFICIAL, appendix [1] |
L03868 | On-chain exploration challenge bonus | 0-5 points | OFFICIAL, appendix [1]; mechanics undefined |
L03869 The official website's English interface text adds a capability framing not present in the PDF: teams are asked to deliver a working financial agent demonstrating "intent understanding, task planning, safe execution, user control and measurable value in a financial scenario," and describes Topic F as "An AI-owned wallet that can spend on-chain" [5]. This is useful guidance for deck structure but is **not** a stated scoring sub-criterion, and should not be presented to judges as one.
L03870 A caution on source hygiene: the same official site still serves an English "Rules" page describing a different competition structure entirely — a blockchain track mandating FISCO BCOS and WeBank open-source components, an AI track scored 70% on submitted attack examples at a primary phase, and a 24 October finals date [5]. That content is stale relative to the 2026 appendix. It must not be cited as current policy, but it is retained below as evidence for one clarification item.
L03871 ### 2.2 Submission package and execution evidence
L03872 Registration and submission share a single deadline of 20 October 2026, 23:59 Beijing time, and submissions may be updated until then with the latest version prevailing [1]. The same deadline is independently reported by the CMU OOU events listing [2], by the IEEE Shenzhen Council announcement [3] and by the Institut Teknologi Sepuluh Nopember call for participation [4], while a Chinese-language aggregator states 16 October [6]; the primary document is preferred in that conflict. The six-part submission checklist is fixed [1]:
L03873 1. **Technical documentation** — system architecture, core algorithm description, security design
L03874 2. **Presentation deck** — no longer than 10 minutes
L03875 3. **Demo video** — optional, no longer than 5 minutes
L03876 4. **Source code** — with README, GitHub repository link, deployment instructions, and test cases
L03877 5. **Security self-assessment report** — "permission-tier implementation and known-risk list"
L03878 6. **Execution evidence** — for non-on-chain topics, simulated-account or sandbox operation logs plus demo video; **for the on-chain topic, real on-chain transaction hashes**
L03879 Two consequences follow directly. First, the requirement is written as "real on-chain transaction hashes," which is the strongest available reading in favour of mainnet execution, though the document does not say "mainnet" explicitly. Second, the security self-assessment explicitly asks for a **permission-tier implementation** description and a **known-risk list**. A submission that produces a well-structured permission-tier table and an honest risk register scores directly against a named deliverable, regardless of bonus. This is the single most under-exploited scoring surface in the whole competition.
L03880 The "real on-chain transaction hashes" line is also the reason a purely simulated or testnet-only submission carries asymmetric risk. Testnet hashes are cheap to produce and easy to dismiss. Testnet-only submissions are not unheard of in adjacent hackathon settings, where teams publish Sepolia addresses and partial transaction hashes as evidence [59], which is exactly the convention a skeptical reviewer may treat as weaker than mainnet. A small real-value mainnet transfer — a few USDC — costs almost nothing, is unambiguous evidence, and can be pointed at directly in the deck. The asymmetry is worth more than the amount at risk.

L03896 ## 3. Agent wallet and agent payment landscape
L03897 ### 3.1 Commercial wallets grouped by policy enforcement locus
L03898 The table below groups the commercial agent wallets inspected by **where a spending constraint is actually evaluated**. This is the axis that separates products that appear similar on a marketing page, and it is the axis a technically literate judge will probe.
L03899 | System | Custody and signing | Where policy is enforced | Verified limitation or boundary | Source |
L03900 |---|---|---|---|---|
L03901 | Coinbase Agentic Wallet (CLI + MCP) | Keys in Coinbase infrastructure; per-session and per-transaction caps; OFAC screening before on-chain submission; networks Base, Base Sepolia, Polygon, Solana, Solana Devnet | Vendor-side control plane plus in-enclave policy per vendor documentation review | Enforcement locus not stated on the public welcome pages inspected; KYT and OFAC screening described as pre-submission | [7][8][55] |
L03902 | Coinbase AgentKit (SDK) | Framework- and wallet-agnostic; Apache-2.0; ~1.3k stars, 521 commits | **None built in** — repository states it does not gate transfers behind human approval, enforce spend caps, or allowlist destinations | Safety is explicitly the developer's responsibility; this is a documented opening, not an oversight | [9] |
L03903 | Privy Agent Wallets | TEE and key sharding; agent-owned wallets or delegated signing; ephemeral signing keys; sessions up to 30 days with token rotation | Policy engine at the infrastructure layer, evaluated before signature; MFA and quorum approval supported | Constraints run off-chain but bind at signing time; dependent on vendor availability | [10][15][62] |
L03904 | Crossmint Agent Wallets | Non-custodial smart wallet; agent added as signer; owner revocable at any time | **On-chain at the wallet level** — transactions outside delegated scope are rejected by the wallet contract | Spend cap, allowed counterparties and time window are the documented controls; no simulation layer described | [11] |
L03905 | Circle Agent Wallets | User-controlled wallets with 2-of-2 MPC; Circle cannot move funds unilaterally | **Off-chain, in Circle's infrastructure, before on-chain submission**; sanctions screening on every transfer | Policy depends on Circle's control plane; gas sponsorship is capped and subject to change | [12][56] |
L03906 | Turnkey | Keys in a secure enclave; default-deny; non-root agent users | **Inside the enclave before a signature is produced**; JSON policies with consensus expressions, function selectors, ABI-level argument control | Agents must never be created as root users, because root bypasses the policy engine | [13] |
L03907 | MetaMask Agent Wallet | Self-custodial, secret recovery phrase held by the user, key export and BYOK supported | Claims guardrails enforced on-chain; Guard mode restricts to allowlisted protocols with daily caps and 2FA on violation | Transaction simulation is listed as "soon", not shipped; threat scanning is Blockaid-powered with a stated coverage cap of $10,000/month | [14] |
L03908 | Fireblocks | Vendor agent payments suite | Policy layer at the infrastructure level, independent of agent and application code | Comparison content is vendor-published | [15] |
L03909 | Binance / OKX Agentic Wallet | Consumer-facing, natural-language driven | App-level rules: daily limits, tradable token ranges, abnormal-transaction handling with auto-reject or second confirmation | Positioned as a retail trading surface; the rules live in the exchange app | [54] |
L03910 The pattern is unambiguous. Only Crossmint and MetaMask make explicit on-chain enforcement claims, and MetaMask's simulation layer is not yet shipped [14]. Privy, Turnkey and Coinbase enforce inside vendor-controlled infrastructure [10][13][55]. Circle enforces off-chain before submission [12]. Every one of these is a legitimate design, and every one of them has a common structural property: **a vendor outage, policy rollback, or account-level compromise sits between the agent and the funds.** That is not a criticism of the vendors; it is the precise definition of the residual risk that a self-contained, contract-enforced boundary removes.
L03911 The one verified capability that matters most for differentiation comes from the opposite direction. Coinbase AgentKit, the most widely adopted on-chain agent SDK, documents in its own repository that it enforces no spend caps, no allowlists and no human approval, and places responsibility for fund safety on the developer [9]. A competition entry that supplies exactly that missing layer, and demonstrates it under attack, occupies an open position rather than a crowded one.
L03912 ### 3.2 Smart account primitives and session keys
L03913 The composable primitives a team would build on are mature and well documented, which materially reduces build risk.
L03914 **Safe** provides an allowance module that lets a non-signer address spend a capped amount of a specific token per period, for example 1 USDC per day, enforced on-chain with no multisig signature required per transfer [16][17]. Safe also ships a delay module, recovery module, a 4337 module, and Guards that execute before and after every transaction and can revert it [18][60]. Zodiac supplies composable Roles and Delay modules from Gnosis Guild [18]. One verified limitation is directly relevant: Safe's own help documentation states that spending limits cap the amount per token but **do not restrict who the spender sends to** [17]. That is a real, citable gap that a recipient-restriction layer would close.
L03915 **ZeroDev Kernel v3** implements ERC-7579 with a composable permission model: `Permission = 1 signer + N policies + 1 action`, with policies for sudo, call (specific contracts, functions and parameters), gas budget, signature scope, rate limit and timestamp window, and revocation by uninstalling the permission plugin through a sudo account [19][20]. In the agent-created flow, the agent generates the key pair, the owner signs the public key, and the agent combines the signed authorisation with its own private key, so the private half is never shared with the owner [66]. ZeroDev supports roughly 30 EVM chains [57].
L03916 **ERC-7579** has become the de facto modular smart account standard, with the four module types validators, executors, hooks and fallbacks, implemented across ZeroDev, Biconomy, Rhinestone, OpenZeppelin and a Safe adapter [21]. **ERC-4337** provides the underlying account abstraction, with over 40 million smart accounts and more than 100 million UserOperations processed by end of 2024 [21]. A useful implementation detail surfaced in practitioner documentation: a Kernel Permission Validator reverts during the **validation** phase, so a policy-violating operation never reaches execution and a sponsored paymaster is not charged [19].
L03917 The practical implication is that a team does not need to invent a wallet. It needs to configure a well-audited one, and add the one or two constraints the audited one lacks.
L03918 ### 3.3 Agent identity, mandates, and payment protocols
L03919 The payment and identity layer is consolidating fast, and the standards themselves describe the gap that remains open.
L03920 **x402** is the HTTP-native stablecoin payment protocol created by Coinbase in May 2025, later stewarded by the x402 Foundation under the Linux Foundation [34]. **AP2**, Google's Agent Payments Protocol, represents each purchase as signed Intent, Cart and Payment Mandates encoded as SD-JWT verifiable credentials, in open and closed form, with v0.2 adding "Human Not Present" execution under pre-authorized instructions; Google donated AP2 to the FIDO Alliance in April 2026 [48][49]. **ACP**, the OpenAI and Stripe Agentic Commerce Protocol, provides agentic checkout with delegated payment via single-use PSP tokens, though OpenAI wound down the Instant Checkout product in March 2026 and kept the protocol with a discovery extension [49].
L03921 The critical structural observation is that these three answer different questions. AP2 proves a human authorized a purchase; ACP governs how an agent interacts with a merchant; x402 moves the money on-chain [49]. None of them constrains what a *wallet-holding agent* may do on its own initiative, which is precisely the Topic F failure mode. None of the mandate systems binds an approval to the exact calldata that eventually executes.
L03922 **ERC-8004** is the on-chain agent identity, reputation and validation standard, still at draft status, with Identity and Reputation registries deployed at the same addresses across more than twenty networks since 29 January 2026, and the Validation registry explicitly not yet deployed [46]. The empirical picture is the interesting part: a study of ERC-8004 across Ethereum, BSC and Base found that only 3%, 4% and 15% of registrations respectively expose a valid registration file with a live service endpoint, that the Reputation registry as deployed cannot function as a trust signal because values are not commensurable and feedback is rarely grounded in verifiable interactions, and that 73.5%, 59.2% and 90.6% of reviewers across the three chains exhibit coordinated Sybil behaviour [47].
L03923 This is a rare, well-sourced result with direct design consequences. Agent identity registries are not currently a usable trust anchor, and a solution that leans on ERC-8004 reputation for a safety decision would be building on sand. The same result argues for the opposite: treat the registry as an **attestation pointer**, and place the trust-bearing weight on a mechanism the team controls and can demonstrate.
L03924 ### 3.4 Academic work on agent authorization
L03925 The academic record is unusually well aligned with this problem, and it defines both what is already solved and what is still open.
L03926 PACE is the closest prior art and the most important citation in this domain [22]. Its contributions are typed transaction intents, a deterministic policy verifier, and signed Policy Decision Records binding intent, policy and simulation report to exact execution bytes with replay and expiration protection, enforced by a Solidity smart account. Evaluation covered 40 tasks across four attack categories plus benign utility, 2,800 trials across 10 seeds, reporting a 0.00 unsafe execution rate and 0.00 false-positive rate on benign tasks against 0.80 for the unguarded baseline, with ablations identifying permissive policy settings (+57.5 percentage points) and the touched-contract allowlist (+12.5 points) as the dominant safety components. The authors frame their own claims as logic-level safety within a reproducible benchmark rather than deployment-ready DeFi security.
L03927 The three gaps in PACE are the opening. It does not model human authorization semantics — who may grant a mandate, under what conditions, with what revocation profile. It does not produce audit evidence a non-technical party can read. And its evaluation is a deterministic sandbox, not a live money-moving agent facing live adversarial input.
L03928 The survey literature supplies the taxonomy and the open problems. The SoK on blockchain agent-to-agent payments systematizes the domain into a four-stage lifecycle of discovery, authorization, execution and accounting, and names the unresolved challenges as **weak intent binding, misuse under valid authorization, payment-service decoupling, and limited accountability** [23]. An analysis of agent-to-agent finance from UCL adds that financial infrastructure must move from account-centric automation to agent-aware automation, where the question is not only whether an account may act but which agent acted, under which mandate, using which wallet permission, on whose behalf, and with what evidence [28]. A companion survey frames the bidirectional relationship between agents and blockchain across identity, permission and delegation, intent and execution, and tokenized agent economies [58].
L03929 On the authorization side, the Cryptographically Verifiable Agent Authorization work formalizes agent authorization as a verifiable relation binding agent principal, authorization request, execution context and policy satisfaction, and identifies as a central open problem the structural separation among identity binding, authorization-request binding, and runtime execution binding [24]. The Authorization Propagation paper argues that multi-agent systems create an authorization problem distinct from prompt injection and not addressed by RBAC, ABAC or ReBAC, deriving seven structural requirements [25]. The Open Agent Passport work characterizes the pre-action authorization gap, noting that agents execute tool calls including fund transfers with no standard mechanism for enforcing authorization before the action executes, and that prompting and filtering cannot substitute [27]. An intent-certificate layer derives short-lived certificates from a trusted request, narrows the authorized tool manifest and checks proposed calls against it, with the explicit caveat that it cannot exceed static policy [26].
L03930 The pattern across all of these is consistent and it is the spine of the recommended design: **prompt-level defences are necessary, insufficient, and the field agrees on this.** OWASP's 2026 LLM Top 10, published 3 August 2026 using a methodology weighting 6,639 documented real-world incidents at 25%, keeps Prompt Injection at number one and moves Excessive Agency from sixth to third, the largest climb on the list [64][65]. OWASP's guidance is explicit that prompting and filtering cannot reliably eliminate the risk alone and that consequential operations need authorization outside the model [64]. The parallel Agentic Applications list names Agent Goal Hijack, Identity and Privilege Abuse, and Memory and Context Poisoning as distinct risk classes [61]. A competition submission that cites these and then demonstrates an architectural control against them is making a mainstream, citable argument, not an idiosyncratic one.

L03952 ## 5. Security incident evidence and its limits
L03953 This section is deliberately conservative, because overclaiming here is the most likely way to lose a skeptical judge who knows the space.
L03954 The Grok/Bankr case is the best-documented AI-specific agent wallet exploit. An attacker reportedly transferred a "Bankr Club Membership" NFT to a Grok-controlled wallet, which acted as a permission-granting object granting expanded tool permissions, then posted a reply asking Grok to translate a Morse-code message whose decoded content instructed a transfer of 3 billion DRB tokens. The reported value was between $155,000 and $174,000, and some funds were later returned [31]. The structural failure is identifiable regardless of the incident's evidentiary status: AI output was treated as a binding financial directive, with no separation between interpretation and execution. Note the reporting posture — Cointelegraph describes the account using qualifying language throughout and states it relies on public discussion in crypto and security communities, with no independent verification claimed [31].
L03955 The Step Finance case must be handled carefully. The company disclosed a breach of treasury wallets on 31 January 2026; CertiK reported 261,854 SOL unstaked and transferred, valued at roughly $27-30 million at the time, and Step later assessed total drained value near $40 million; approximately $4.7 million was recovered; the company, SolanaFloor and Remora Markets wound down in late February 2026 [32][33]. Both CoinDesk and crypto.news state that investigators clarified the smart contract infrastructure was not breached and the vulnerability stemmed from compromised executive devices [32][33]. Secondary commentary recasting this as an AI agent permissions failure is not supported by the primary reporting, and an incident archive explicitly notes that the primary article never mentions AI [29]. The defensible lesson is still real and still useful: **device and endpoint compromise remains the dominant operational risk for any treasury**, autonomous or not. But it should not be presented as evidence about agent safety specifically.
L03956 The malicious LLM router study is the strongest supply-chain evidence and has a widely misreported number. Researchers from UC Santa Barbara, UC San Diego, Fuzzland and World Liberty Financial tested 428 routers, 28 purchased and 400 free, and found 9 actively injecting malicious code into agent tool calls, 17 accessing researcher-planted AWS canary credentials, 1 draining ETH from a researcher-controlled private key, and 2 using adaptive evasion triggers that stayed dormant until a condition was met [29][30]. The paper introduces payload injection and secret exfiltration as core attack classes, with dependency-targeted injection and conditional delivery as evasion variants, and names the structural cause: routers terminate TLS and act as application-layer proxies with full plaintext access, and no provider enforces cryptographic integrity between the client and the upstream model [29]. The "$500,000" figure circulating widely is attributed by CoinDesk to a client incident reported by the researchers; the controlled experiment drain was under $50 [29][30].
L03957 Against these three, the invoice-embedded injection class is both better documented and better suited to a competition submission, because it is not dependent on any single disputed incident. Barracuda's October 2026 research on phishing emails carrying hidden instructions identified four recurring concealment techniques — HTML comments, CSS-invisible text, Base64-encoded data and zero-width characters — and described a hidden block in an invoice email that told a summarizing AI to add a fake priority action changing vendor payment details, nudging an employee toward wiring money to the attacker [43]. The InjecAgent benchmark evaluated 30 tool-integrated agents across 1,054 indirect injection cases, with a ReAct-prompted GPT-4 agent vulnerable in 24% of attacks, and the earlier EchoLeak zero-click indirect injection against Microsoft 365 Copilot is tracked as CVE-2025-32711 [44]. Check Point's PromptGuardX demonstration used a standard-looking invoice containing a hidden instruction, with no PDF vulnerability involved, succeeding only because an AI system read the instruction and had the right tool [63]. Practitioner threat write-ups consistently converge on the same conclusion: the defence that works is architectural, scoping credentials to least privilege, keeping money-movement decisions in deterministic code outside the model, and logging every tool call so a manipulated transaction can be proven or ruled out [45].
L03958 The evidentiary conclusion is precise and useful: **invoice-borne indirect injection is the attack class with the strongest documentation, the clearest financial impact, and the most natural fit for an autonomous on-chain payment agent.** The other incidents are supporting colour, and should be cited carefully or not at all.

L03960 ## 6. Commercial reality of agent payments
L03961 Any concept whose value proposition depends on agent payment volume being large inherits a serious factual problem. Agentic payments on Base crossed 100 million cumulative transactions in roughly three quarters, rising from near zero in Q3 2025, with growth moderating in early 2026 as speculative activity cooled. Chainalysis found much of the growth driven by meme coin farming rather than agents buying services, and found that transactions of $1 or more rose to 95% of volume while the 10 cent to $1 band collapsed from 46% to 4% [34].
L03962 The organic-volume estimates are far below the headline counts. As of 21 April 2026, Visa and Artemis reported that x402's raw totals of $135.7 million across 178.3 million transactions collapsed to $15.0 million and 109.6 million transactions once wash-trading heuristics were applied, with roughly 89 percent of headline volume being non-organic [29]. Artemis reported approximately $28,000 in genuine daily volume at an average payment near $0.20, with about half of observed transactions attributable to self-trading or wash trading [29]. Separately, market analysis reported x402 daily settlement volume down 93% year to date, with a seven-day average near $41,800 and a provisional daily figure around $28,400 [35]. By early July 2026, third-party trackers cited cumulative x402 transactions of 191,751,598 across 12 chains, with Stripe and Tempo's Machine Payments Protocol at 46,198 cumulative events [53].
L03963 The pattern across sources is consistent even where the specific numbers conflict: protocol traffic is large, organic commercial volume is small, and the two have been diverging since late 2025. A submission that pitches "the agent economy needs micropayments" is pitching against data that does not support it, and any judge who follows payments coverage will know.
L03964 The design consequence is direct. A Topic F entry should not depend on micropayment volume for its value story. It should target a workflow where a single transaction has enough value that the payment is worth securing, and where the failure being prevented is measured in thousands rather than cents. Cross-border supplier settlement, milestone escrow, and invoice payment sit in that band. API micropayment does not, which is why it appears in this analysis as an infrastructure component rather than as a use case.

L04040 ## 10. Reference architecture and threat model
L04041 The recommended architecture separates eight concerns, and the separation is the substance of the submission rather than a diagram. The governing principle is that the LLM never holds authority; it proposes, and a contract disposes.
L04042 1. **AI planning.** The model reads the task and the untrusted document set, and emits a typed intent: payee address, token, amount, chain, expiry, and the human-readable reason. The model has no signing capability and no credential of any kind.
L04043 2. **Trusted policy enforcement.** A deterministic engine evaluates the intent against a policy set that the human authored: allowed chains, allowed tokens, allowed payees or payee-resolution rules, per-transaction and per-period caps, and required conditions. This runs outside the model.
L04044 3. **Transaction simulation.** The operation is simulated against current chain state, returning balance deltas, approval changes, storage changes and the call trace. Tenderly provides this as a JSON-RPC method and REST API across more than 98 networks, with production state fidelity [41], and exposes exposure and balance-delta inspection specifically for approvals [42].
L04045 4. **Transaction authorization.** The simulation result and the intent are compared. A mismatch between what was intended and what would occur blocks the operation. Above a configured threshold, a human approves; the approval is a signed artefact, not a notification.
L04046 5. **Protected signing.** The approved bytes are signed inside the protected boundary. The agent process never holds a key capable of an unauthorized transfer.
L04047 6. **Blockchain execution.** Execution occurs through a smart account with a composable permission, using the `Permission = 1 signer + N policies + 1 action` model from ZeroDev Kernel v3 or a Safe allowance module [19][16]. Revocation is a permission-plugin uninstall or a module disable, executable by a human without the agent's cooperation.
L04048 7. **Receipt verification.** The receipt is checked against the intent: token, amount, recipient, and chain. Confirmation depth follows an amount-tiered policy rather than a fixed wait. Base documents a four-stage ladder — flashblock at roughly 200ms, L2 block at roughly 2s, L1 batch at roughly 2 minutes, L1 finality at roughly 20 minutes — with flashblock reorganisation below 0.001% and a single observed L2 reorg representing about 0.0000003% of transactions [36][37].
L04049 8. **Tamper-evident audit evidence.** A hash-committed decision record binds intent, policy version, simulation result and authorization decision, so a third party can verify after the fact that the executed bytes matched the approved intent.
L04050 The threat model below maps the principal attack classes onto a control. The controls in the "independent of the LLM" column are the ones that hold even if the model is fully compromised, which is the property OWASP argues for when it states that consequential operations need authorization outside the model [64].
L04051 | Threat | Control | Independent of the LLM |
L04052 |---|---|---|
L04053 | Direct prompt injection via user text | Typed intent schema; unrecognised fields rejected | Yes |
L04054 | Indirect injection via invoice, email, or document | Payee and amount resolved only from a trusted channel; document content treated as data, never as instruction [43][45] | Yes |
L04055 | Injection via tool output or API response | Output schema validation; no free-text to transaction path | Yes |
L04056 | Malicious LLM router rewriting tool calls [29][30] | Signer verifies calldata hash against a locally computed intent before signing | Yes |
L04057 | Intent-to-bytes substitution between simulation and signing | Nonce, expiry, and calldata hash bound into the authorization artefact [22] | Yes |
L04058 | Unauthorized recipient substitution | Payee allowlist or a payee-resolution rule anchored to a pre-registered vendor record, closing the gap Safe's allowance module leaves open [17] | Yes |
L04059 | Amount manipulation | Per-transaction and per-period caps evaluated by the policy engine and the account contract [11][13] | Yes |
L04060 | Wrong chain or wrong token | Chain and token allowlists in the account policy [19] | Yes |
L04061 | Token approval exploitation | Simulation exposes approval and allowance changes before signing [42]; an approval change blocks unless explicitly permitted | Yes |
L04062 | Duplicate payment or replay | Intent nonce and expiry; account-level replay protection [22] | Yes |
L04063 | Reentrancy | Reuse of audited account contracts rather than custom execution logic | Yes |
L04064 | Private key exposure | Key isolation in a protected signer; the agent holds no transferable key | Yes |
L04065 | Policy modification by the agent | Only a human-controlled sudo path installs or removes permissions [19] | Yes |
L04066 | Privilege escalation via capability object | Explicit deny on unsolicited inbound asset-driven permission changes; the Grok/Bankr case is the reference incident [31] | Yes |
L04067 | Front-running and slippage | Slippage bounds in the intent; MEV-aware execution where a swap is involved [14] | Yes |
L04068 | Misreported success | Receipt verified against intent; finality policy by amount [36][37] | Yes |
L04069 | Reorganisation | Amount-tiered confirmation depth plus a settlement watcher that revalidates released payments | Yes |
L04070 | Compromised vendor infrastructure | Contract-enforced policy survives vendor outage; the vendor holds no unilateral authority over funds | Partially |
L04071 | Gas exhaustion | Gas policy in the account permission [19] | Yes |
L04072 | Endpoint compromise of an operator | Wallet dedicated to agent operation, separate from operator treasury; the Step Finance case is the reference [32][33] | Yes |
L04073 The maximum possible loss under a fully compromised agent is bounded by construction: the sum of the per-period cap, plus any funds held in a bounded escrow, plus nothing else — because the account contract itself refuses to execute outside policy. That is a provable statement about a bounded set of funds, and it is the statement a judge can check by reading the account configuration rather than by trusting a claim.
L04074 Recovery and emergency stop are deliberately simple. A human can uninstall the permission plugin or disable the module without the agent's participation [19][16]; a pause flag in the account contract halts execution while leaving revocation available; and because every intent is hash-committed before execution, a disputed transfer can be adjudicated against a record the agent cannot alter after the fact.

L04076 ## 11. Architectures for three shortlisted candidates
L04077 ### 11.1 Bounded-Authority Payment Agent
L04078 The recommended build composes C1 and C2. An agent receives supplier invoices, extracts candidate payment details, and is required to pay. The attacker model is an adversary who controls the document contents. The agent's extraction is treated as an untrusted proposal; the payee, token, amount and chain are resolved by a deterministic policy engine that consults a pre-registered vendor record rather than the document itself, and any instruction-shaped text in the document is inert because it never reaches a component that can act on it.
L04079 On-chain, a ZeroDev Kernel v3 account or a Safe smart account carries a permission of one signer, a call policy restricted to an approved set of targets and selectors, a gas policy, a rate-limit policy and a timestamp window [19][20]. The decision record commits the intent hash, the policy version hash, the simulation result hash and the authorization decision before execution. A published adversarial suite runs a fixed set of attacks against the running agent and publishes every transcript: obfuscated instruction payloads in invoice fields, capability-object privilege escalation, replayed intents, altered amounts, wrong-chain substitution, and policy-modification attempts.
L04080 Measured outcomes are the deliverable: attack count, blocked count, escapes, and the maximum value that moved during the campaign. Success is a zero-escape result with a non-zero attack count, which is a far stronger claim than "we built a safe wallet" because it is falsifiable and the transcripts are public.
L04081 Implementation assumptions: a payment API or simulated invoice corpus is needed for the demo; a real supplier relationship is not. Mainnet execution uses a small real-value transfer on Base, with Base Sepolia retained as the reproducible test environment [38][40].
L04082 ### 11.2 Budget Envelope Treasury
L04083 The safest implementable design. An SME or protocol treasury issues per-cost-center envelopes with period resets, each enforced in the account contract, each optionally restricted to a recipient set. Because Safe's allowance module caps amounts but does not restrict recipients [17], the envelope adds a recipient dimension, which is a genuine, citable functional gap rather than a feature invention.
L04084 Autonomy is genuine within envelopes: the agent sweeps, reallocates between envelopes, and settles routine obligations without approval. Above a threshold, the Safe multisig or a Turnkey-style consensus policy applies [13][16]. The risk-control story is containment, the demonstration is budget adherence across a period, and the measurable metric is envelope breach count, which is zero by construction and therefore verifiable by a third party reading the contract.
L04085 This is the fallback if the security demonstration becomes too ambitious for the window. It scores lower on innovation and roughly the same on the other two axes, and it is the concept to fall back to rather than the concept to lead with.
L04086 ### 11.3 Attestation-Gated Escrow
L04087 The most original and the highest-risk option. It combines agent escrow settlement, which has real prior art including a reference implementation of Intelligent AI Delegation deployed on Base Sepolia with verified transactions [50], with a counterparty attestation gate that is genuinely novel, and is motivated by an empirical finding: ERC-8004 reputation as deployed cannot function as a trust signal, with 73.5% to 90.6% Sybil behaviour across three chains and only 3% to 15% of registrations exposing a live endpoint [47].
L04088 The design treats the registry as an attestation pointer rather than a trust source, and requires a signed behavioural record before release. The risk is straightforward: the verification source is the hard dependency, and if it cannot be bootstrapped without prior history, the concept collapses. The upside is that it targets a real financial problem with genuine multi-party stakes, and that no inspected product combines conditional settlement with counterparty verification in one contract.

L04106 ## 13. Adversarial case against the leading candidate
L04107 The strongest case against the Bounded-Authority Payment Agent runs as follows, and it should be taken seriously rather than answered with reassurance.
L04108 **It is a compliance project wearing a financial costume.** Task completion carries 40%. A judge who spends ten minutes watching a system refuse to pay an invoice may conclude that nothing financial actually happened. The counter is that the system must also demonstrably pay legitimate invoices on-chain, with real hashes, in the same demo, which converts it from a firewall demonstration into a payment demonstration. Without that, the critique lands.
L04109 **Its mechanism is published prior art.** PACE appeared on 18 August 2026, roughly two months before this competition [22]. Any judge tracking agent security literature may recognise the intent-binding pattern, and claiming it as an original contribution would be a credibility failure. The defence is to cite PACE explicitly as prior art the build adopts, and to claim only the adversarial record, which PACE does not provide and which no vendor provides.
L04110 **The 7-day window is the real risk.** A custom smart account, a policy engine, a simulation gate, an agent, an adversarial suite, a deck, a video, a security self-assessment and real mainnet execution is a large amount of work. The failure mode is shipping something broken, which scores near zero on the dominant 40%. The mitigation is to vendor the account rather than build it, to defer C8 and C21 entirely, and to treat the adversarial suite as the first thing built, not the last, because it is the differentiator and it is the thing most likely to need iteration.
L04111 **Accountability evidence is hard to show in ten minutes.** A hash-committed decision record is technically sound and visually invisible. A judge may not register it. The mitigation is to make the audit record human-readable by design, showing the intent, the policy clause that governed it, the simulation delta, and the decision, in a form that can be read on a slide.
L04112 **The security framing can read as fear-mongering.** The incident evidence is thinner than the narrative suggests, as established in section 5. A submission that overclaims AI agent hacks invites a challenge it cannot survive. The mitigation is to lead with the well-documented invoice-injection class [43][44][45] and to present the agent incidents as corroboration with their reporting status stated.
L04113 **Regulatory framing carries exposure.** A system that pays suppliers autonomously could be read as facilitating payment fraud. The mitigation is to frame it as fraud *defence*, to make the blocked-payment path the headline of the demo, and to avoid any design that looks like an instruction for redirecting payments.
L04114 **Finality claims are easy to overstate.** Calling a 2-second L2 inclusion "settled" is technically imprecise given Base's own four-stage ladder [36][37]. A judge who knows the difference will notice. The mitigation is to state the confirmation tier used and why, which turns a weakness into evidence of rigour.
L04115 **The competitor scenario is under-modelled.** Other teams may submit a trading bot with a live PnL dashboard, which is visually impressive and familiar. The counter is that trading is saturated by at least four major platforms [7][14][54] and that a seven-day PnL claim invites unjustified scrutiny, whereas containment is verifiable by anyone.
L04116 **Vendor dependence is a real critique of the incumbent pattern, and the same critique applies here.** If the account relies on a vendor bundler, RPC or paymaster, the failure modes documented for LLM routers [29][30] apply at the infrastructure layer. The mitigation is to make the enforcement point the on-chain contract and to state explicitly which dependencies are trusted and why, which is itself a scored artefact given the security self-assessment requirement [1].
L04117 The case against the leading candidate is strong enough that the recommendation should be presented with the control experiment attached. A containment claim that has never been shown to fail when containment is removed is not a finding.

L04119 ## 14. Feasibility inside the submission window
L04120 ### 14.1 Stack selection
L04121 **TypeScript over Python** for the agent and policy layer, because the strongest primitives — ZeroDev permissions [19], ERC-7579 [21], Tenderly SDK [41] — are TypeScript-native, and because the agent frameworks with the widest tool coverage are TypeScript-first. Python remains reasonable if the team is stronger there, at the cost of integrating across language boundaries under time pressure.
L04122 **Foundry over Hardhat** for contract work, given faster compilation and built-in fuzzing, which matters when the security self-assessment asks for a permission-tier implementation and a known-risk list [1].
L04123 **Base mainnet for evidence, Base Sepolia for reproduction.** Base Sepolia is chain ID 84532 with free faucets delivering test ETH, and test USDC is available from Circle's faucet [38]. The Coinbase Developer Platform Node provides a free tier of 10 million billing units per month, but documentation states that starting January 2026 the API requires a payment method on file, which is a real onboarding friction to schedule early [39]. The Coinbase paymaster sponsors up to $15,000 monthly on mainnet and is unlimited on testnet [40].
L04124 **Tenderly for simulation** rather than building it, available over JSON-RPC and REST across more than 98 networks with balance-change, exposure-change and state-change inspection [41][42]. This converts the single largest engineering risk into an integration task.
L04125 **The account layer should be vendored, not built.** A Kernel v3 account with a composable permission [19][20] or a Safe with allowance and guard modules [16][17][18] gives audited contracts, a documented revocation path, and recipient-restriction that Safe's allowance module alone does not provide.
L04126 **The agent framework is close to irrelevant to the score.** What matters is that the model proposes and cannot dispose. Any framework that supports structured output and tool calls suffices, and spending time on framework selection is a mistake.
L04127 ### 14.2 Week-one schedule
L04128 Day 1 is accounts, dependencies and the vendor decision, because the payment-method requirement for Coinbase infrastructure [39] is the most likely day-one blocker. Days 2 and 3 are account deployment, permission configuration, and a single working real transfer on Base, which de-risks the evidence requirement early. Day 4 is the policy engine and the simulation gate. Day 5 is the adversarial suite, built before the demo polish, because it is the differentiator and it is the thing most likely to need iteration. Day 6 is the full end-to-end run, receipts, and the audit record. Day 7 is documentation, deck, security self-assessment, and submission, with the deadline at 23:59 Beijing time [1].
L04129 This schedule is tight and assumes a team of three to five working in parallel, which the rules permit [1]. It also assumes the concept is scoped to C1 plus C2 with no C8 or C21 work at all.

L04131 ## 15. Validation experiments
L04132 Three experiments are decisive. Each is designed so that a negative result is informative rather than merely disappointing.
L04133 **Experiment 1: injection containment under live adversarial input.** Hypothesis: a policy engine that resolves payee, token, amount and chain from a trusted channel blocks obfuscated instruction payloads embedded in invoice fields. Prototype: a running agent on Base Sepolia with a corpus of 50 invoices, 20 carrying hidden instructions using the concealment techniques documented by Barracuda [43]. Acceptance: zero payments to a non-allowlisted address across all 20. Failure: any single escape. Negative tests: legitimate invoices must still pay, otherwise the control is useless. Expected evidence: transaction hashes, policy decisions, and the blocked-attempt log.
L04134 **Experiment 2: intent-to-bytes binding under substitution.** Hypothesis: an authorization artefact that commits the calldata hash prevents a different transaction from being executed under an approval. Prototype: intercept the flow between simulation and signing and substitute a modified call. Acceptance: the modified call is rejected at the contract or at the signer. Failure: the substituted call executes. This is the experiment that decides whether the concept's central claim is true, and it is the experiment most likely to be asked about by a judge.
L04135 **Experiment 3: real-value end-to-end settlement.** Hypothesis: the full pipeline produces a verifiable mainnet transfer and a matching receipt within the window. Prototype: one legitimate invoice paid on Base mainnet with a small real amount. Acceptance: a mainnet transaction hash plus a receipt that matches the intent on token, amount, recipient and chain, with a stated confirmation tier [36]. Failure: no hash, or a receipt mismatch. This experiment protects the 40% and should be run first despite being listed third, because its failure invalidates everything.
L04136 A fourth experiment is worth running if time permits: a control condition in which the policy engine is disabled and the same adversarial suite is replayed, producing escapes. This converts a containment claim into a measured result with a baseline, and it is the single most persuasive artefact available within this concept.
L04137 No experiment described here has been executed. All are technically plausible but untested, and should be described that way in any submission.

L04139 ## 16. MVP scope and roadmap
L04140 The minimum viable version that remains distinguishable from the complete vision is narrow by design. It includes one real financial workflow, being supplier invoice payment. It includes a smart account with contract-enforced per-transaction and per-period caps, a call policy restricted to approved targets, and a timestamp window. It includes a deterministic policy engine that resolves payment details from a pre-registered vendor record rather than from the document. It includes simulation gating with an intent comparison. It includes a hash-committed decision record and a human-readable rendering of it. It includes a published adversarial suite with transcripts and a control condition. And it includes real mainnet evidence.
L04141 Deliberately excluded from the minimum version: autonomous trading, yield strategies, DeFi positions, cross-chain rebalancing, agent identity registration, streaming payroll, and any self-revocation mechanism. Each of these is a separate project with its own failure modes, and each dilutes the one story the submission is trying to tell.
L04142 The longer-term roadmap, aimed at the Grand Final on 21-23 November 2026 and beyond [1], has three stages. The first extends the adversarial suite from invoice-borne injection to tool-output and memory-context poisoning, which are the other two classes named in the OWASP agentic list [61], and adds the control condition across all classes. The second extends the policy engine into a chain-agnostic kernel with a conformance test suite, so that a policy defined once provably behaves identically across chains [21]. The third adds attestation-gated conditional settlement, using the empirical weakness of the ERC-8004 registry as design input rather than as infrastructure [47].

L04144 ## 17. Open research questions
L04145 Several questions remain genuinely unresolved and are worth stating as open rather than as confident positions.
L04146 Whether on-chain intent binding is the right place to spend the gas is unsettled. PACE measures 29,826 to 31,822 gas of overhead [22], which is negligible against the value of a prevented loss, but the comparison against a well-configured account abstraction stack has not been published at scale.
L04147 Whether a simulation gate in the critical path is fast and accurate enough in practice is empirically open. Tenderly documents the capability [41] but not its failure rate under adversarial conditions, and simulation is only as good as the state it runs against.
L04148 Whether self-revocation is a real control or a security theatre question is unresolved. An agent detecting its own compromise is a strong assumption, and no inspected commercial product markets it, which is evidence of caution rather than of impossibility.
L04149 Whether the ERC-8004 ecosystem can produce a usable trust signal is currently answered in the negative by the available evidence [47], but registries change, and the result is a measurement of the current deployment rather than of the design.
L04150 Whether judges will read a security demonstration as financial value is not a research question but it is the largest strategic uncertainty, and it should be addressed by demonstration design rather than by argument.
L04151 Whether the challenge bonus rewards anything a team can deliberately target is unknown, and the honest position is to not target it while asking the clarification question.
